### Overview & Clinical Procedure
A Coronary Angiogram (also referred to as coronary arteriography or cardiac catheterization) is an invasive diagnostic imaging procedure that utilizes fluoroscopic X-rays and radiopaque contrast dye to visualize the lumen diameter, flow dynamics, and structural patency of the coronary arteries that supply oxygenated blood to the myocardium.

### Left Main Coronary Artery (LMCA)
Arises from the left aortic sinus and bifurcates into the Left Anterior Descending (LAD) and Left Circumflex (LCx) arteries. Significant narrowing in the LMCA affects blood flow to a large portion of the left ventricle and carries significant prognostic weight.

### Left Anterior Descending (LAD) Artery
Courses down the anterior interventricular groove to supply the anterior myocardial wall, apex, and anterior two-thirds of the interventricular septum. Often nicknamed the "widowmaker" when severely narrowed proximally. Major side branches include Diagonal branches (D1, D2) and Septal perforators.

### Left Circumflex (LCx) Artery
Traverses the left atrioventricular groove to supply the lateral and posterior left ventricular myocardium. Major side branches include Obtuse Marginal (OM1, OM2) branches.

### Right Coronary Artery (RCA)
Originates from the right aortic sinus and supplies the right atrium, right ventricle, inferior left ventricular wall, and the sinoatrial (SA) and atrioventricular (AV) nodal conduction tissue in most individuals. Branches include Acute Marginal branches, the Posterior Descending Artery (PDA), and Posterior Lateral branches (PLB). Stenosis in the RCA often causes inferior myocardial ischemia or conduction slowing.

### Coronary Artery Stenosis (Percentage Narrowing)
Coronary artery narrowing (stenosis) is quantitatively expressed as a percentage reduction in vessel luminal diameter compared with an adjacent angiographically normal reference segment:
- **< 50% Luminal Diameter Reduction (Mild / Non-obstructive)**: Usually does not restrict baseline coronary blood flow or cause exercise-induced myocardial ischemia. Primarily managed through aggressive lifestyle modifications, statin therapy, and cardiovascular risk factor optimization.
- **50% to 69% Luminal Diameter Reduction (Moderate Stenosis)**: Borderline hemodynamic significance. May cause angina symptoms during high physical exertion. Often evaluated further with physiologic tests such as Fractional Flow Reserve (FFR) or Instantaneous Wave-free Ratio (iFR).
- **70% to 99% Luminal Diameter Reduction (Severe / Hemodynamically Significant Stenosis)**: Restricts coronary flow reserve, frequently precipitating effort angina or ischemic myocardial strain. Clinical guidelines recommend evaluation by a cardiovascular specialist for potential revascularization (Percutaneous Coronary Intervention / PCI with stent placement, or Coronary Artery Bypass Graft / CABG) alongside medical therapy.
- **100% Luminal Diameter Reduction (Total Occlusion)**: Complete cessation of antegrade blood flow through the native coronary lumen, characteristic of acute myocardial infarction or chronic total occlusion (CTO).

### Treatment and Revascularization Guidelines
- **Optimal Medical Therapy (OMT)**: Antiplatelet agents, lipid-lowering therapies (statins), beta-blockers, and ACE inhibitors to prevent atherosclerotic progression.
- **Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI / Angioplasty & Stenting)**: Minimally invasive catheter-guided balloon dilation and drug-eluting stent placement to restore vessel caliber in focal severe obstructions.
- **Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG)**: Surgical revascularization utilizing arterial (e.g., internal mammary artery) or venous conduits to bypass diffuse multivessel or complex Left Main coronary disease.
- **Patient Communication Rule**: Angiogram findings require comprehensive correlation with symptoms (chest tightness, shortness of breath, fatigue), non-invasive stress testing, and ventricular function. Patients should always discuss their exact angiographic percentages and personalized treatment options directly with their cardiologist.
